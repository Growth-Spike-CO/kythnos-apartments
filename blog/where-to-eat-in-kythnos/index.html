Where to Eat in Kythnos

Written by Vrettos Gkoumas · 2026-06-18

Kythnos keeps its cooking honest. You will not find fusion experiments or Instagram-chasing presentations here. What you will find is Cycladic food done the way families have cooked it for generations: simple ingredients, careful preparation, and flavours that make sense together.

The island's tavernas are seasonal. Most open in May and close by October. Nearly all are family-run. A grandmother might still be rolling out pastry in the back while her son grills fish at the front.

What to Eat

Start with sfougato. This is the island's signature dish: a baked courgette and cheese mixture, somewhere between an omelette and a pie, golden on top and soft inside. Every kitchen makes it slightly differently. Some add more herbs. Some bake it thicker. I have never had a bad one.

Ask for thermiotiko loukaniko if you see it on the board. This is the local sausage, named after Thermia, the island's old name. The meat is usually pork, seasoned with orange peel and sometimes a little wine. It comes grilled, often with chips or a plain tomato salad.

Lamb and goat here taste of thyme because that is what the animals graze on. The meat is tender and faintly herbal. Spring and early summer are the best months for it.

Fresh fish and seafood land at two ports: Merichas and Loutra. What you get depends entirely on what came in that morning. Red mullet, sea bream, squid, octopus. Prices vary with the catch. If the sea has been rough for a few days, there might be nothing fresh at all.

Finish with something sweet. Amygdalota are soft almond sweets, sometimes shaped into little pears or dusted with icing sugar. Local thyme honey is dark and aromatic. You will see jars of it for sale in the villages.

Chora (Messaria)

Chora sits inland, on a ridge. The main square and the streets around it hold several tavernas. Tables spill onto the pavement. In summer the square fills up after nine in the evening.

We tell our guests to walk through Chora at dusk and see which kitchens are busy. A full taverna is usually full for a reason. Look for places where Greek families are eating. That is always a good sign.

The cooking here tends toward grills and oven dishes. Sfougato appears on most menus. So do stuffed vegetables in summer: tomatoes and peppers filled with rice and herbs.

Dryopida

Dryopida is quieter. Fewer tourists make it up here, though the village is worth the trip for its own sake. The tavernas are small and the pace is slower.

I like eating here when I want to sit for a long time without feeling rushed. The food is straightforward. Expect moussaka, slow-cooked lamb, local cheeses. Nothing fancy. Just good.

Merichas

Merichas is the port, so this is where the fish comes in. Tavernas line the waterfront. Tables sit almost at the edge of the harbour. At sunset every seat fills up.

The fish here is as fresh as it gets. Grilled whole, drizzled with lemon and olive oil, served with horta or a plain salad. Simple. If you want seafood, eat it in Merichas or Loutra.

Arrive early if you want a table by the water, especially in July and August. Or book ahead. We always do.

Loutra

Loutra sits on the northeast coast. It is smaller than Merichas but also has a working harbour. Fishing boats tie up here. The tavernas are right on the water.

This is the other place to eat fresh fish. The setting is lovely: calm water, a scattering of tamarisk trees, a thermal spring that bubbles up by the rocks.

Loutra feels more relaxed than Merichas. Fewer day-trippers. The pace is gentler. I come here when I want to eat slowly and watch the light change over the bay.

Kanala

Kanala is a small fishing village on the southwest coast. A church sits on a rocky outcrop. A narrow beach curves around the bay. A handful of tavernas face the water.

The sunset here is beautiful. Tables fill up as the light starts to go golden. People come for the view as much as the food, but the cooking is solid: grilled meats, fried squid, greek salads with good feta.

Kanala is quieter than Merichas. If you want to sit by the sea without the port bustle, this is your place.

A Few Practical Notes

Menus are often handwritten on a board. Sometimes there is no written menu at all. The owner will tell you what is ready. Do not expect things to be available if they are crossed off the board.

Service can be slow. This is not rudeness. Kitchens are small and everything is cooked to order. Bring patience. Bring good company. Enjoy the wait.

Cash is useful. Most places take cards now, but not all. I always carry cash just in case.
